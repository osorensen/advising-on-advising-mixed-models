Estimation

Given \(b \sim N(0, G)\) and \(\epsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^{2} I)\), \(\hat{\beta}\) and \(\hat{b}\) solve the penalized least squares problem

\[ \min_{\beta,\, b} \; \lVert y - X\beta - Zb \rVert^{2} + \sigma^{2}\, b^{T} G^{-1} b \]

Estimation

Henderson’s mixed model equations (Henderson 1975)

\[ \begin{bmatrix} X^{T}X & X^{T}Z \\ Z^{T}X & Z^{T}Z + \sigma^{2} G^{-1} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \hat{\beta} \\ \hat{b} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} X^{T}y \\ Z^{T}y \end{bmatrix} \]

  • The ridge penalty on \(b\) makes the system nonsingular
  • But the penalty depends on \(G\) and \(\sigma^{2}\), which are unknown

Marginal Likelihood

Integrate out the random effects

\[ L(\beta, \sigma^{2}, G) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}} \underbrace{p(y \mid b;\, \beta, \sigma^{2})}_{N(X\beta + Zb,\; \sigma^{2} I)}\, \underbrace{p(b;\, G)}_{N(0,\; G)} \; \mathrm{d}b \]

Closed form: \(y \sim N(X\beta,\, ZGZ^{T} + \sigma^{2} I)\)

  • \(b\) is integrated out: maximize over \(\beta\), \(\sigma^{2}\) and \(G\) alone

What About Missing Data?

With one grouping factor, the likelihood is a product over participants

\[ L(\beta, \sigma^{2}, G) = \prod_{i=1}^{m} \int p(y_{i} \mid b_{i};\, \beta, \sigma^{2})\, p(b_{i};\, G)\, \mathrm{d}b_{i} \]

with \(y_{i}\) the \(n_{i} \geq 1\) observations participant \(i\) has

  • We integrate over unobserved \(b_{i}\), not over missing \(y\): no imputation
  • All 306 observations are used, including 20 participants with one measurement
  • Valid if missing at random given the model (Rubin 1976), e.g., dropout depending on earlier observed \(y\)

Where Does the Misconception Come From?

  • Early software used the EM algorithm, which treats \(b\) as missing data and fills in its conditional expectations in the E-step (Laird and Ware 1982)
  • Predicted random effects \(\hat{b}_{i}\) look like imputed values
  • Missing covariates are different: lmer() drops those rows, losing their observed \(y\), so multiple imputation may be needed

Maximum Marginal Likelihood

With \(\theta = (\sigma^{2}, G)\) and \(V_{\theta} = ZGZ^{T} + \sigma^{2} I\)

\[ \begin{aligned} &-2 \ell_{\text{ML}}(\beta, \theta) = \\ & n \log 2\pi + \log \lvert V_{\theta} \rvert + (y - X\beta)^{T} V_{\theta}^{-1} (y - X\beta) \end{aligned} \]

For given \(\theta\), the maximizer over \(\beta\) is the GLS estimate

\[ \hat{\beta}_{\theta} = \left(X^{T} V_{\theta}^{-1} X\right)^{-1} X^{T} V_{\theta}^{-1} y \]

  • Plug in \(\hat{\beta}_{\theta}\) and optimize the profile likelihood over \(\theta\) only

Restricted Maximum Likelihood

Integrate out \(\beta\) too (Harville 1974)

\[ L_{\text{REML}}(\theta) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}} L(\beta, \theta) \, \mathrm{d}\beta \]

\[ \begin{aligned} & -2 \ell_{\text{REML}}(\theta) = \\ &-2 \ell_{\text{ML}}(\hat{\beta}_{\theta}, \theta) + \log \lvert X^{T} V_{\theta}^{-1} X \rvert - p \log 2\pi \end{aligned} \]

  • ML ignores that \(\beta\) is estimated: variance components biased downward, like \(\text{RSS}/n\) vs. \(\text{RSS}/(n - p)\)
  • Matters with few clusters or many fixed effects

REML and Model Comparison

REML likelihoods are not comparable

  • Likelihood ratio tests, AIC, BIC involving fixed effects: use ML
    • anova() in lme4 refits REML models with ML automatically
  • Same fixed effects, different random effects: REML is fine

Inference in Practice

Fixed effects

  • lmer() reports no p-values: with unbalanced data the null distribution of the \(t\) statistic, and its degrees of freedom, are not known exactly
  • Small samples: Satterthwaite (lmerTest) or Kenward–Roger (pbkrtest) degrees of freedom, based on REML (Kenward and Roger 1997)

Confidence intervals

  • confint(mod) gives profile likelihood intervals, also for the standard deviations and correlations; Wald intervals are poor for variance parameters
  • Parametric bootstrap when in doubt: confint(mod, method = "boot").

Testing Variance Components

  • \(H_{0}: \sigma_{b}^{2} = 0\) is on the boundary, so the LRT statistic is \(\tfrac{1}{2}\chi^{2}_{0} + \tfrac{1}{2}\chi^{2}_{1}\), not \(\chi^{2}_{1}\): halve the naive p-value
  • Adding a correlated random slope: \(\tfrac{1}{2}\chi^{2}_{1} + \tfrac{1}{2}\chi^{2}_{2}\)
  • The mixtures are asymptotic; with few clusters, use the parametric bootstrap

Is there a random slope for \(x\)? Same fixed effects, so REML is fine

mod0 <- lmer(y ~ x + (1 | id), data = dat)
lrt <- as.numeric(2 * (logLik(mod) - logLik(mod0)))
c(naive = pchisq(lrt, df = 2, lower.tail = FALSE),

Generalized Linear Mixed Models

Marginal Likelihood

\(y\) distributed according to an exponential family with mean \(\mu\), and linear predictor

\[ g(\mu) = \eta = X \beta + Z b \]

Integral no longer known

\[ L(\beta, \phi, G) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}} \underbrace{p(y \mid b;\, \beta, \phi)}_{\text{exp. fam.}}\, \underbrace{p(b;\, G)}_{N(0,\; G)} \; \mathrm{d}b \]

Common Integral Approximations

  • Penalized quasi-likelihood (Breslow and Clayton 1993): very fast but crude. Implemented in MASS::glmmPQL().
  • Laplace approximation (Tierney and Kadane 1986): very good for \(\beta\) but typically biased for \(G\). Implemented in lme4::glmer() and glmmTMB::glmmTMB().
  • Gaussian quadrature (Pinheiro and Chao 2006): useful if \(G\) is a scalar and you care deeply about it.

Laplace Approximation

Write the integrand as \(e^{h(b)}\), with

\[ h(b) = \log p(y \mid b;\, \beta, \phi) + \log p(b;\, G) \]

Second-order Taylor expansion of the exponent around the mode \(\hat{b}\), where \(\nabla h(\hat{b}) = 0\):

\[ h(b) \approx h(\hat{b}) - \frac{1}{2} (b - \hat{b})^{T} H (b - \hat{b}) \]

with \(H = -\nabla^{2} h(\hat{b}) = Z^{T} W Z + G^{-1}\) and \(W\) the diagonal GLM weights at \(\hat{b}\)

Laplace Approximation

The integrand is now a Gaussian kernel

\[ \begin{aligned} L(\beta, \phi, G) &\approx e^{h(\hat{b})} \int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}} e^{-\frac{1}{2} (b - \hat{b})^{T} H (b - \hat{b})} \, \mathrm{d}b \\ &= e^{h(\hat{b})} \, (2\pi)^{q/2} \, \lvert H \rvert^{-1/2} \end{aligned} \]

Taking \(-2 \log\):

\[ \begin{aligned} -2 \ell(\beta, \phi, G) \approx\; & \underbrace{-2 \log p(y \mid \hat{b};\, \beta, \phi) + \hat{b}^{T} G^{-1} \hat{b}}_{\text{penalized deviance}} \\ & + \log \lvert G \rvert + \log \lvert Z^{T} W Z + G^{-1} \rvert \end{aligned} \]

Same Form as the LMM

For the LMM, \(h(b)\) is exactly quadratic in \(b\), so the formula is exact, with

\[ -2 \log p(y \mid \hat{b};\, \beta, \sigma^{2}) = n \log 2\pi\sigma^{2} + \frac{\lVert y - X\beta - Z\hat{b} \rVert^{2} }{\sigma^{2}} \]

\(W = I / \sigma^{2}\), and \(\hat{b}\) the penalized least squares solution from before

  • GLMM: approximate, with \(\hat{b}\) the conditional mode
  • Accurate when \(p(b \mid y)\) is close to Gaussian; worse with few, binary observations per cluster

Finding the Conditional Mode

Penalized iteratively reweighted least squares (PIRLS) for given \(\beta\), \(G\), from \(b^{(0)} = 0\):

  1. \(\eta = X\beta + Zb^{(k)}\), \(\mu = g^{-1}(\eta)\)
  2. \(z_{i} = (Zb^{(k)})_{i} + (y_{i} - \mu_{i})\, g'(\mu_{i})\), \(\; w_{i} = 1 / \{\phi\, V(\mu_{i})\, g'(\mu_{i})^{2}\}\)
  3. Solve \(\left(Z^{T} W Z + G^{-1}\right) b^{(k+1)} = Z^{T} W z\)
  4. Repeat until the penalized deviance converges
  • Each step is the LMM problem, with weights \(W\) and response \(z\)
  • Runs inside every evaluation of the Laplace likelihood; an outer optimizer handles \(\beta\) and \(G\)

Conditional vs. Marginal Effects

\[ E(y \mid b) = g^{-1}(X\beta + Zb), \qquad E(y) = \int g^{-1}(X\beta + Zb)\, p(b)\, \mathrm{d}b \neq g^{-1}(X\beta) \]

  • Identity link: the two coincide, so \(\beta\) is also the population-averaged effect
  • Other links: \(\beta\) is a subject-specific effect, and the population-averaged curve is flatter

Probit vs. Logit

Random intercept \(b \sim N(0, \sigma_{b}^{2})\), linear predictor \(\eta = x^{T}\beta\), and \(\varepsilon \sim N(0, 1)\) independent of \(b\)

Population-averaged effect with Probit:

\[ E\{\Phi(\eta + b)\} = P(\varepsilon - b \leq \eta) = \Phi\left(\eta \big/ \sqrt{1 + \sigma_{b}^{2}}\right) \]

Logit, approximate, using \(\operatorname{logit}^{-1}(t) \approx \Phi(t / 1.7)\):

\[ E\{\operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\eta + b)\} \approx \operatorname{logit}^{-1}\left(\eta \big/ \sqrt{1 + 0.346\, \sigma_{b}^{2}}\right) \]

  • Probit: the marginal model is again a probit model, with coefficients \(\beta / \sqrt{1 + \sigma_{b}^{2}}\)
  • Logit: the GLMM odds ratio is a within-patient odds ratio, not a population odds ratio
  • With random slopes the scaling depends on the covariates, for both links
  • Population-averaged effects: average predictions over \(b\), or fit GEE

Numerics and Implementation Details

Sparse Matrices

Imagine we follow students over time, nested within schools, and that students may change school.

mod_school <- lmer(y ~ x + (x | id) + (1 | school), data = dat_school)

In nlme, crossed effects need a dummy grouping factor spanning all observations

dat_school$all <- factor(1)
lme(y ~ x, data = dat_school,
    random = list(all = pdIdent(~ 0 + school), id = ~ x))

Sparse Matrices in lme4

Packages

  • lme4 (Bates et al. 2015): sparse matrices; Laplace approximation; probably the fastest?
  • glmmTMB (Brooks et al. 2017): sparse matrices; Laplace approximation; automatic differentiation; easily handles lots of different distributions.
  • brms (Bürkner 2017): lme4 syntax; Stan backend; generates Stan code; very flexible.
  • nlme: historically important; maintained but not actively developed.

Numerical Instability

Singular Fits

boundary (singular) fit: see help('isSingular')

  • The estimate is on the boundary: a variance of zero or a correlation of \(\pm 1\)
  • A property of the likelihood for these data, not a software bug
  • Typical with few clusters, few observations per cluster, or many variance parameters

Henderson’s equations need \(G^{-1}\), which then does not exist. lme4 instead writes \(b = \Lambda_{\theta} u\) with \(u \sim N(0, \sigma^{2} I)\) (Bates et al. 2015)

\[ \min_{\beta,\, u} \; \lVert y - X\beta - Z\Lambda_{\theta} u \rVert^{2} + \lVert u \rVert^{2} \]

so a singular \(\Lambda_{\theta}\) causes no problems

Convergence Warnings

Model failed to converge with max|grad| = 0.006 (tol = 0.002)

  • The outer optimizer over \(\theta\) (and \(\beta\) for GLMMs) stopped where the gradient is not quite zero
  • Often a false positive, especially in large data sets (see ?lme4::convergence)
  • Check: allFit(mod) refits with all available optimizers; if the estimates agree, report them

Model is nearly unidentifiable: large eigenvalue ratio - Rescale variables?

  • Center and scale covariates, e.g., time in decades or centered at its mean
  • Don’t ask for more random effects than the data can support

Binary Outcomes in Small Data

40 patients with 2 binary measurements each, true random intercept SD 1.5, 200 simulated data sets

Laplace underestimates the SD (median 1.12 vs. 1.52 with quadrature). Singular fits occur with both methods (Laplace 4%, quadrature 4%): they come from the likelihood, not the approximation.

What Can We Do?

  • Treat a singular fit as information: these data cannot distinguish the variance from zero
  • Simplify the random effects: drop correlations with (x || id), or drop random slopes
  • More accurate integral: glmer(..., nAGQ = 25), available with a single random intercept
  • Bayesian fit with brms: the posterior shows the full uncertainty about the SD, instead of a point estimate at zero

Take-Home Messages

  • No imputation: the random effects are integrated out
  • Missing outcomes: fine under MAR; missing covariates: not
  • REML for variances; ML to compare fixed effects
  • Small binary data: check Laplace against quadrature
  • GLMM coefficients are subject-specific
  • Singular fits and warnings tell you about the data
